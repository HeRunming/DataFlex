\begin{abstract}
Targeted instruction selection assumes that training subsets better aligned
with a small query set should yield greater downstream utility.
We introduce directional second-moment matching, an exact greedy coreset
objective that matches a query set in projected-gradient geometry, and show in
controlled MMLU experiments that the second-order representation improves over
the first-order target representations we study.
Yet stronger target matching does not reliably translate into stronger task
performance: on held-out BBH, \method{} attains the closest target geometry
while underperforming Random selection, a reversal observed with both
Llama-2-7B and Llama-3.2-3B model stacks.
On the same query examples used to define the targeting signal, target-aware
methods also improve the pipeline's operational differentiable surrogate
without corresponding gains in the chain-of-thought task metric.
These results show that successfully optimizing target-gradient alignment is
not sufficient for downstream utility, and motivate selection objectives whose
intermediate targets are validated against the quantities ultimately used to
evaluate the model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Instruction tuning draws from increasingly large and heterogeneous pools, while
a deployment often requires only a narrower set of capabilities.
Targeted instruction selection addresses this mismatch by using a small query
set to identify candidate examples that should be most useful for a target
task. Recent methods operationalize usefulness through semantic retrieval,
gradient similarity, influence approximations, target subspaces, or
task-specific rewards
\citep{xia2024less,kang2024getmore,wang2025nice,min2026gist}.
Across these formulations lies an intuitive surrogate assumption:
\emph{if a selected subset matches the target more faithfully, training on it
should improve target utility}.

This paper directly tests that assumption. Doing so requires more than observing
that one selector loses to another: a weak downstream result could simply mean
that the selector failed to optimize the target representation it was meant to
match. We therefore construct a deliberately measurable target-matching
instrument. Given unit-normalized projected gradients $u$, we represent a set
by its directional second moment,
\begin{equation}
    M_P = \mathbb{E}_{u\sim P}[uu^\top].
\end{equation}
Under the quadratic kernel $k_2(u,v)=(u^\top v)^2$, empirical maximum mean
discrepancy (MMD) is exactly the squared Frobenius distance between directional
second moments. Our method, \emph{Directional Second-Moment Coreset}
(\method{}), greedily minimizes this set-level discrepancy. It consequently
lets us verify whether target geometry is actually optimized rather than
inferring success from downstream performance.

The experiments reveal a positive result followed by a sharper negative one.
In controlled MMLU stress tests, the second-order representation improves over
the first-order alternatives we examine, and at a 5\% data budget \method{}
consistently outperforms the targeted baselines under our shared protocol.
This establishes \method{} as a useful instrument rather than a deliberately
weak straw man. However, the advantage is not an advantage over target-agnostic
sampling: \method{} is indistinguishable from Random in observed mean
performance at 5\% and trails Random at 1\%.

We then evaluate on held-out BBH queries and examples drawn from the same task
family. Here the reversal becomes explicit. Across three query/selection draws,
\method{} obtains the lowest second-moment discrepancy in every draw, yet loses
to Random in every draw. The direction repeats on a second model stack:
Llama-2-7B shows a $-2.94$ percentage-point \method{}--Random gap and
Llama-3.2-3B a smaller $-0.89$ point gap. Thus the geometric objective did not
merely fail to optimize; it optimized successfully without delivering utility.

Finally, we locate the break more precisely. On the \emph{same 64 query items}
used to define the selection signal, target-aware methods reduce the wrapped
final-answer cross-entropy computed by the targeting pipeline, while
chain-of-thought exact match does not improve. Query-to-test shift therefore
cannot by itself explain the result. A serialization diagnostic narrows the
claim: the behavior of bare-context cross-entropy differs across model stacks,
so we do not claim that cross-entropy is generically misaligned with
chain-of-thought evaluation. The supported conclusion concerns the exact
operational surrogate used by the selection pipeline.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate targeted selection as matching the directional second
    moment of unit projected gradients and derive an exact greedy MMD coreset
    rule. This provides a target-matching objective whose success can be
    measured directly.
    \item We separate representation from selection in controlled MMLU
    experiments. Directional second moments improve the studied targeted
    selectors, while the extra coreset benefit is budget dependent.
    \item We give an instrumented empirical counterexample to the target-matching
    assumption. On BBH, better target-gradient geometry and a better operational
    query surrogate do not imply better task utility, and the direction is
    observed on two model stacks.
    \item We report negative controls and scope limits---Random and no-SFT
    references, a matched-format control, equal-step training, same-item
    diagnostics, serialization checks, and contamination audits---rather than
    interpreting the reversal as a uniquely identified mechanism.
\end{itemize}

\section{Target Matching with Directional Second Moments}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Targeted instruction selection}

Let $\mathcal{C}=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be a candidate instruction pool and
$\mathcal{Q}=\{q_j\}_{j=1}^{M}$ a small target query set. Given a budget $K$,
the goal is to select $\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{C}$, $|\mathcal{S}|=K$, such
that supervised fine-tuning on $\mathcal{S}$ improves held-out target
performance.

Following gradient-based targeted selection
\citep{xia2024less,nayak2026critical}, we compute per-example gradients at a
shared warm-up checkpoint and project them to a reusable low-dimensional
datastore. For an example $z$, let
\begin{equation}
    u(z) = \frac{\Pi \widetilde g(z)}
                 {\|\Pi \widetilde g(z)\|_2},
    \qquad \|u(z)\|_2=1,
\end{equation}
where $\Pi$ is a shared Rademacher projection. The main protocol follows the
LESS-aligned asymmetry: candidate gradients are Adam-aware, while query
gradients are raw SGD gradients. All statements below concern the resulting
unit vectors, not unprojected parameter-space gradients.

\subsection{Why a directional second moment?}

First-order mean-gradient matching represents a target by
$\mathbb{E}[u]$. This signed mean can cancel when useful examples occupy
opposite directions, even if they share the same underlying orientation.
We instead use the non-centered directional second moment
\begin{equation}
    M_P = \mathbb{E}_{u\sim P}[uu^\top].
    \label{eq:moment}
\end{equation}
Because $uu^\top=(-u)(-u)^\top$, this representation captures orientation
rather than sign. We deliberately call it a \emph{directional second moment},
not a covariance: the vectors are normalized and the mean is not subtracted.

Consider the positive semidefinite kernel
\begin{equation}
    k_2(u,v) = (u^\top v)^2.
\end{equation}
Since
$\langle uu^\top,vv^\top\rangle_F=(u^\top v)^2$, the biased empirical MMD
between a selected set $\mathcal{S}$ and query set $\mathcal{Q}$ is exactly
\begin{align}
    \operatorname{MMD}_{k_2}^2(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Q})
    &=
    \left\|
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}u_su_s^\top
    -
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}u_qu_q^\top
    \right\|_F^2 \nonumber\\
    &\equiv \dsecond(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Q}).
    \label{eq:mmd_identity}
\end{align}
The identity is algebraic: \method{} matches this empirical second moment
exactly as defined, but the kernel is not characteristic and does not identify
the full gradient distribution.

\subsection{Exact greedy coreset construction}

For a candidate $x$, define target relevance and accumulated selected-set
similarity as
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathcal Q}(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal Q|}
    \sum_{q\in\mathcal Q}k_2(u_x,u_q),
    \quad
    r_{\mathcal S}(x)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}k_2(u_x,u_s).
\end{equation}
At step $m=|\mathcal S|$, the exact one-step marginal for minimizing the biased
MMD selects
\begin{equation}
    x^\star =
    \arg\max_{x\notin\mathcal S}
    \left[
    r_{\mathcal Q}(x)
    -
    \frac{r_{\mathcal S}(x)+k_2(u_x,u_x)/2}{m+1}
    \right].
    \label{eq:greedy}
\end{equation}
For unit vectors $k_2(u_x,u_x)=1$. The first term rewards query alignment and
the second penalizes selected-set self-similarity. We use the deterministic
exact marginal throughout; we make no stochastic-greedy or submodularity
guarantee.

\paragraph{Role in this paper.}
\method{} is not claimed to optimize downstream utility. Its purpose is to
construct a strong, measurable intervention on target geometry. If
\method{} lowers $\dsecond$ but utility fails to improve, the discrepancy
cannot be attributed to the geometry objective being left unoptimized.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Candidate pool, features, and training}

The candidate pool contains 270,679 processed examples from Flan v2
(100,000), CoT (100,000), Dolly (15,011), and OASST1 (55,668), matching the
pool used by LESS-style targeted selection. For each model stack, a LoRA warm-up
checkpoint is used to construct a candidate-gradient datastore. We use an
8,192-dimensional Rademacher projection with seed 123, unit-normalize the
projected vectors, and generate query-gradient caches separately for each draw.
The selected indices are materialized into static subsets; all downstream
comparisons restart from the corresponding base model rather than continuing
the warm-up run.

Downstream SFT uses LoRA rank 128, alpha 512, dropout 0.05 on
\texttt{q/k/v/o\_proj}, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, linear decay, 3\%
warm-up, bfloat16, effective batch size 128, and four epochs. The 5\% budget is
$K=13{,}533$ (420 optimizer steps) and the 1\% budget is $K=2{,}707$ (84
steps). Full configurations, prompt templates, hashes, and data-processing
details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\subsection{Target families}

\paragraph{MMLU controlled stress test.}
We construct ten globally disjoint 64-example query sets:
five STEM-majority draws (51 STEM, 13 humanities) and five
humanities-majority draws with the proportions reversed. Evaluation is balanced
accuracy over held-out MMLU STEM and humanities subjects
\citep{hendrycks2020mmlu}. Draw index is paired with SFT seed
$\{42,1,2,3,4\}$; for comparisons shared across the two skew directions, the
primary descriptive unit is the five direction-averaged draw-index blocks.

\paragraph{BBH external validation.}
We stratify 27 BIG-Bench Hard tasks into a query reservoir and a frozen held-out
split \citep{suzgun2022bbh}. Three 64-example query draws define the selection
signals; the held-out evaluation contains 5,209 examples and uses pinned
chain-of-thought few-shot exact match. Each query/selection draw is crossed
with two SFT seeds, which are averaged \emph{within} the draw. The statistical
unit is therefore the draw ($n=3$), not the six seed-level runs.

\subsection{Comparators and reporting policy}

The controlled gradient baselines are: first-order mean-gradient TopK
(LESS-style), first- and second-order relevance TopK, first- and second-order
round-robin (RR), GIST whitening on the shared projected datastore
(GIST-SharedProj), and a task-metric adaptation of NICE
(NICE-MMLU-EM). Because several are shared-protocol adaptations rather than
full official end-to-end reproductions, we use these qualified names in the
paper. Target-independent controls are uniform Random-$K$, length-matched
Random on MMLU, a sequence/label-length-matched Random control on BBH, and a
shared no-SFT reference.

The study is descriptive. We report draw-level means, paired differences, and
win counts; we do not report significance tests over $n=3$ or $n=5$ blocks.
Subtask breakdowns and individual SFT seeds are diagnostics, not independent
replicates.

the gap. It is not a causal decomposition: the control changes several
correlated properties simultaneously. Full results are in

\section{Where the Surrogate Chain Breaks}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

\subsection{The target geometry is optimized}

For every BBH draw, we compute the exact discrepancy optimized by \method{},
\begin{equation}
    \dsecond(\mathcal S,\mathcal Q_d)
    = \|M_{\mathcal S}-M_{\mathcal Q_d}\|_F^2.
\end{equation}
On Llama-2, mean $\dsecond$ is 0.0863 for \method{} and 0.1098 for Random;
on Llama-3.2 it is 0.0644 and 0.0752. More importantly, \method{} has the
lowest $\dsecond$ in all three draws of both stacks. Better geometry is even
associated with lower accuracy within each draw: the descriptive Spearman
correlation between $\dsecond$ and accuracy is positive in every draw
(lower $\dsecond$ is better). The same sign remains after restricting the
Llama-2 comparison to target-aware methods, although the ranking samples are
too small for inferential claims.

This rules out the narrow explanation that \method{} loses because its
objective was not actually optimized. In MMLU, \method{} is also closer than
Random to a leave-one-draw-out balanced reference in all ten draws at both
budgets (Appendix~\ref{app:geometry}). Thus the observed failure is not simply
that \method{} follows a skewed query away from the balanced reference.

\subsection{The operational query surrogate improves}

Gradient-based targeting uses a differentiable final-answer objective to
construct query gradients. We therefore recompute teacher-forced final-answer
cross-entropy on the same query items, under the exact wrapped serialization
used by each stack's targeting pipeline. On Llama-2, all four target-aware
methods reduce this loss by 0.68--1.40 nats, while Random increases it by 0.33.
On Llama-3.2, the target-aware methods reduce it by 3.19--3.40 nats and Random
by only 1.37. The second stack therefore gives a magnitude split rather than a
sign split; in both, the targeted methods move the operational surrogate more.

\subsection{Task utility does not follow on the same examples}

Comparing query loss to held-out exact match changes both metric and examples.
We consequently evaluate chain-of-thought exact match on the same 64 query
items. For Llama-2, every target-aware method's mean query exact match falls
relative to base ($-3.65$ to $-6.51$ points) despite the substantial
cross-entropy reductions. On Llama-3.2, all three targeted methods again have
negative mean changes, with \method{} falling by 6.51 points; Random changes by
$+1.30$ points. The individual 64-item draw metrics are coarse---one item is
1.56 points---but the mean separation is consistent with the held-out ranking.

The same-item result matters logically. A pure account in which targeted
selection improves the query examples but fails to generalize cannot explain
the observation, because the task metric does not improve on the query examples
themselves. It does not rule out every form of overfitting, nor does it identify
the missing mechanism.

\subsection{Serialization narrows the claim}

The operational query loss uses each stack's LlamaFactory wrapper, whereas BBH
generation uses a pinned bare evaluation context. Recomputing final-answer
cross-entropy on that bare context yields different behavior across stacks. On
Llama-2, no method improves bare-context CE, although targeted methods degrade
it much less than Random. On Llama-3.2, target-aware methods do improve
bare-context CE while Random does not. Therefore we do \emph{not} claim that
final-answer cross-entropy is inherently misaligned with chain-of-thought
generation. The claim is narrower and operational: the surrogate actually used
by the targeting pipeline can improve on the defining examples without a
corresponding improvement in the task metric, and the role of serialization is
model-stack dependent.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Instruction data selection.}
General-purpose instruction selection commonly scores data for quality,
difficulty, diversity, or representativeness. DEITA combines complexity and
diversity for data-efficient alignment, while principled warm-up selection
studies how small subsets affect later fine-tuning
\citep{liu2024deita,kang2024getmore}. Our focus is narrower: selection for a
specific target described by a small query set.

\paragraph{Targeted and influence-based selection.}
LESS builds reusable low-dimensional gradient datastores and uses
optimizer-aware gradient similarity for targeted instruction tuning
\citep{xia2024less}. NICE replaces next-token-loss influence with a
policy-gradient construction tied to non-differentiable evaluation metrics
\citep{wang2025nice}. GIST recovers a target-gradient subspace and scores
candidate alignment under coupled optimization geometry
\citep{min2026gist}. A recent controlled study separates representations from
selection algorithms and finds that gradient representations are often
predictive, but rankings vary across tasks, models, and budgets
\citep{nayak2026critical}. We differ by directly optimizing a measurable
set-level geometry and then verifying that the optimized geometry itself fails
to imply utility.

\paragraph{Surrogate mismatch and strong Random baselines.}
ROSE motivates reward-oriented selection by documenting non-monotonicity
between instruction-tuning loss and task reward \citep{wu2025rose}; NICE makes
a related move from differentiable training loss to task metrics
\citep{wang2025nice}. Large-scale studies also find that sophisticated
selectors can be difficult to distinguish from or can underperform Random
\citep{xia2025random}. Our contribution is not another broad selector
leaderboard and not a replacement objective. It is a diagnostically resolved
counterexample chain: target geometry is measurably optimized, the operational
query surrogate moves in the intended direction, and task utility still does
not follow.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The study covers two model stacks (Llama-2-7B and Llama-3.2-3B), one candidate
pool, and two target families. A model stack changes model, tokenizer, and
serialization together; it is not an architecture-only ablation. BBH has only
three independent query/selection draws, and the effect on the 3B stack is
small. MMLU couples draw index to SFT seed, so direction-averaged blocks rather
than ten cells carry comparisons shared across directions. Several baselines
are controlled adaptations on a common datastore, not full official
end-to-end reproductions. The contamination audit includes exact, fuzzy, and
long n-gram lexical checks but not a semantic nearest-neighbor audit. Finally,
the diagnostics do not identify a unique mechanism. Format and provenance are
plausible contributors, while a simple task-frequency specialization account
is unsupported, but neither conclusion is causal.

\section{Conclusion}

Within these limits, the evidence rejects a common sufficiency assumption.
We made target matching stronger, verified that the selected sets were closer
under the optimized geometry, and found that downstream utility still did not
follow. Target-aware selectors can accomplish their intermediate objective
without accomplishing the task objective. Progress in targeted instruction
selection therefore requires not only better optimization of a chosen
surrogate, but stronger validation that the surrogate mediates the utility we
ultimately care about.

\section{Method Derivations}
\label{app:derivations}

\subsection{MMD identity}

Let
$\widehat M_{\mathcal S}=|\mathcal S|^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}u_su_s^\top$
and define $\widehat M_{\mathcal Q}$ analogously. Expanding the squared
Frobenius norm gives
\begin{align}
\|\widehat M_{\mathcal S}-\widehat M_{\mathcal Q}\|_F^2
&=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal S|^2}\sum_{s,s'}
\langle u_su_s^\top,u_{s'}u_{s'}^\top\rangle_F \nonumber\\
&\quad+
\frac{1}{|\mathcal Q|^2}\sum_{q,q'}
\langle u_qu_q^\top,u_{q'}u_{q'}^\top\rangle_F \nonumber\\
&\quad-
\frac{2}{|\mathcal S||\mathcal Q|}\sum_{s,q}
\langle u_su_s^\top,u_qu_q^\top\rangle_F \\
&=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal S|^2}\sum_{s,s'}(u_s^\top u_{s'})^2
+\frac{1}{|\mathcal Q|^2}\sum_{q,q'}(u_q^\top u_{q'})^2
-\frac{2}{|\mathcal S||\mathcal Q|}\sum_{s,q}(u_s^\top u_q)^2.
\end{align}
This is the biased empirical MMD under $k_2$.

\subsection{Greedy marginal}

Terms depending only on $\mathcal Q$ can be dropped. Comparing the objective
after adding $x$ to a set of size $m$ yields Equation~\ref{eq:greedy} after
positive rescaling. The implementation maintains $r_{\mathcal S}(x)$
incrementally and masks selected or invalid rows. Zero and non-finite feature
rows are rejected before selection.
